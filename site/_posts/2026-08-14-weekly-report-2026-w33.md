Two strands moved this week: a pipeline for aligning long recordings to their transcripts when almost no training data exists, and a study of how speech recognisers trained on tidy read speech hold up against the messiness of real conversation.

## [Minimally Supervised Endangered Language ASR](/projects/#minimally-supervised-endangered-language-asr)

Much of the work went into the Interspeech 2026 paper, whose central diagram now lays out the alignment pipeline end to end. Force alignment is the task of placing a known transcript onto the audio timeline — deciding which stretch of sound corresponds to which word. It is the groundwork for training a recogniser from long recordings: once you know where each word falls, you can cut the audio into labelled fragments the model can learn from.

The pipeline threads a transcript and a long recording through a single decoder. The text side is normalised and turned into narrow language models that expect only the words actually spoken; the audio side is reduced to standard acoustic features. The decoder reconciles the two using a pronunciation lexicon and a seed acoustic model. The contribution is in how those last two pieces are built. Where the standard recipe leans on an English pronunciation dictionary and a large pre-built acoustic model, neither of which exists for an endangered language, my version substitutes a graphemic lexicon derived straight from the normalised text and an acoustic model bootstrapped from a small amount of short-form recordings, growing from a simple monophone model up to a triphone one. In other words, it manufactures the two resources a low-resource language lacks out of the little data that is on hand.

## Other

The domain-adaptation work asks a blunt question: recognisers are usually trained on clean, read speech, so how badly do they stumble on spontaneous speech, and can adapting the audio at test time close the gap?

Answering it first meant pinning down what "spontaneous" actually means as a measurement. I characterised thirteen English corpora along that axis, drawing a sixty-minute sample from each and transcribing it automatically (WhisperX with large-v3) with word-level alignment. Three families of cue separate loose speech from careful speech: speaking rate in words per minute; pausing, in how often speakers pause, how long the pauses run, and what fraction of the time is silence; and discourse markers — the *uh*, *um*, *like*, *you know*, and *I mean* that pepper unrehearsed talk — counted per hundred words. The word error rate in the last column is not a result about the corpora but a rough gauge of how far the automatic transcript can be trusted.

The discourse-marker count draws the sharpest line. Read corpora such as TIMIT and LibriSpeech sit near zero, while telephone and meeting conversation — Switchboard, AMI, the Edinburgh accented-speech corpus — carry two to three and a half markers per hundred words, ten to twenty times as many. Speaking rate and pausing tell a looser but consistent version of the same story.

With the axis defined, the core experiment fine-tunes self-supervised recognisers (wav2vec2, HuBERT, WavLM) on a hundred hours of read LibriSpeech and tests them across that continuum, from read speech at one end to spontaneous at the other, with and without a voice-conversion step that rewrites the test audio into a voice the model finds easier. Two large multilingual models are carried along untuned as reference points. The results table for this comparison is still filling in, so I have no numbers to report on it yet.

Most of the week's engineering went into the voice-conversion component, which I am still reproducing and debugging. A probe into whether the model's learned acoustic prior carries information that a simpler prior already supplies confirmed one premise of the study and pushed a competing explanation up the priority list. A sweep over the conversion model's noise schedule gave a first concrete diagnostic point: over 330 conversion pairs, the arm with the widened schedule reached a naturalness score of 1.16, speaker similarity of 0.64, and word and character error rates of 86% and 78% — quality still far short of what the method should deliver, which is the point of the diagnosis rather than a discouragement. Alongside these I added batch evaluation for a second conversion model, a matched-clip control, and seed-controlled repeat runs to establish how much of the metric movement is just noise.

I also reorganised the running diagnosis of why the reproduction underperforms into two levels: high-level problems with the experiment's design, controls, and framing, and specific mechanistic causes that each map to a code fix. The design problems come first, because several of the specific fixes are downstream of design questions that are still open and may not survive their resolution.
