Most of this week went into two threads: a figure for the Interspeech paper on aligning speech in endangered languages, and a run of experiments testing whether making read speech sound spontaneous helps a recogniser understand real conversation. The short answer to the second question turned out to be no.

## [Endangered Language ASR](/research/#endangered-language-asr)

Work on the Interspeech paper centred on the diagram that explains how the system lines up a long recording with its written transcript. The task is *forced alignment*: given an audio file and a text that is known to be correct, mark exactly when each word is spoken. This matters for languages with little recorded data, because once speech and text are aligned you can cut a long, messy recording into clean training clips.

The diagram sets my approach against the standard recipe of Panayotov and colleagues from 2015. Both pipelines take two inputs — the transcript and the waveform — and end at the same decoding step that produces the alignment. The transcript passes through text normalisation and a set of *biased language models* (statistical models tuned to expect the words actually in the transcript, which keeps the aligner from drifting). The waveform is turned into MFCCs, a compact numerical summary of the sound. The two approaches differ in the knowledge they feed the decoder. The baseline relies on an off-the-shelf English pronunciation dictionary and a pre-built acoustic model trained on Voxforge. Mine instead builds a *graphemic lexicon* straight from spelling — useful when no pronunciation dictionary exists for the language — and bootstraps its own acoustic model from short, easy-to-align clips, training first a simple monophone model and then a richer triphone model from it.

## Other

### Does converting read speech to spontaneous speech help recognition?

The largest block of work tested a single idea for domain adaptation. Recognisers trained on clean, carefully read speech stumble on spontaneous conversation, which is faster, more hesitant, and full of filler. The idea was to take read training recordings and use *voice conversion* — resynthesising the audio so it carries the rhythm and disfluencies of conversation — and then train on this altered speech, hoping it would prepare the model for the real thing.

To ground the comparison I first measured how spontaneous thirteen English speech collections actually are, sampling an hour from each and computing speaking rate in words per minute, the frequency and length of pauses, and the rate of *discourse markers* — the *uh*, *um*, *like*, *you know* and similar fillers that pepper unscripted talk. Word error rate (WER) against each collection's own transcript serves as a rough confidence check on the automatic measurements. The collections span a clear continuum, from clipped read sentences to loose conversation.

The pattern is what one would expect: read collections such as TIMIT and LibriSpeech sit near one filler per five hundred words, while conversational collections such as Switchboard (SWBD) and meeting recordings (AMI) carry ten to twenty times as many, speak faster, and pause longer.

With that scale in place, I built the conversion model itself — a diffusion-based voice converter in the Grad-TTS family, whose decoder solves a stochastic differential equation to generate the output spectrogram. One version is trained jointly from scratch to turn read speech into spontaneous speech. Among the candidates, the chosen model reached an intrinsic score of 24.1 against a close alternative's 23.9, a gap whose confidence interval spans zero — so the two are, for practical purposes, indistinguishable in quality.

The augmentation experiments then delivered a consistent and negative verdict. Adding converted LibriSpeech to the clean training set made recognition *worse*, not better, by 2.22 WER on conversational meeting speech and 3.24 on held-out data. Widening the test to ten collections and several model families confirmed it: a wav2vec2 recogniser trained on the converted speech reached 53.48 WER on meeting speech, against 45.87 for the plain read baseline, and was worse on all ten sets. Converted speech hurt everywhere.

The follow-up asked why. Of the several explanations considered, the evidence pointed to one main cause — *label noise*: the conversion process garbles the audio enough that it no longer faithfully matches its transcript, so the model is partly trained on wrong answers. The competing explanations were ruled out. A correlation between predicted naturalness and character error rate on the converted clips supports this reading: the conversions that sound worst are also the ones whose content has drifted.

### Venture research

Separately, I pushed forward the desk research for a prospective software venture on the Isle of Man, surveying economic problems beyond regulated finance that software and AI might address. The idea list was reopened and three candidates were worked up, each with a kill test and a cheapest next test: an AI customer-service and job-booking assistant for Island small businesses; a tool that turns bundles of old Manx property deeds into first-registration applications (roughly 71% of the Island's land is unregistered, and the government's own estate project has stalled since 2021); and an automated patient-contact service for Manx Care's waiting lists, which held 17,833 open outpatient referrals in May 2026. Each was logged with its risks — notably that none has yet been tested with a buyer.
