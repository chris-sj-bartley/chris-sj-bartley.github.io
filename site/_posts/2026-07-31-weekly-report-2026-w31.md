This week I chased one question: why every voice-conversion model I train on my own constructed pairs still trails the model DiffVC ships with. I wrote up the candidate causes with evidence, assembled the results into a "ladder" that varies how much speech each training pair spans, and ran the full reproduction of the best arm across ten speakers. The picture is now sharp. Short training units break the model outright, and even the best long-unit arm leaves a real, unexplained gap.

## Experiments

Voice conversion takes a recording of one person and re-renders it in another person's voice while keeping the words intact. I work with DiffVC, a diffusion-based converter, and I have been retraining it on pairs of utterances I build myself rather than on its stock self-sampling scheme. The open question is how the *unit* of a training pair — a single word, a short run of phones, a longer run of phones — changes what the model learns.

To answer it I built a ladder of arms that differ only in that unit length, from `word_n1` (single words) up to `phone_n14` (spans of fourteen phones), and measured each against `vc_libritts_wodyn.pt`, the released baseline. I then put the best arm, `phone_n14`, through the full reproduction pipeline: 330 conversions spread over ten LibriTTS target speakers, each scored on three axes. UTMOS is an automatic naturalness score on the usual 1–5 mean-opinion scale (higher is better). SIM is speaker similarity, the agreement between the converted voice and a real reference of the target (0–1, higher is better). CER is the character error rate of an automatic transcript of the output — how garbled the words came out (lower is better).

Alongside the ladder I tidied the A/B comparison table, dropping one arm that was not applicable and another that had regressed from over-training, and added a control reference curve. I recorded the whole picture in a new analysis document, `BASELINE_GAP.md`, whose remit was deliberately narrow: name what needs fixing, and stop short of proposing the fixes.

## Results

The ladder is monotonic — the longer the unit, the better every metric — and it splits cleanly into two regimes:

| Model | UTMOS | SIM | CER% |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline (stock self-sampling, 110 ep) | 2.98 | 0.901 | 4.58 |
| phone_n14 (best arm) | 2.85 | 0.844 | 14.99 |
| phone_n7 | 2.76 | 0.832 | 36.95 |
| word_n3 | 2.53 | 0.797 | 67.62 |
| word_n1 | 1.36 | 0.613 | 90.94 |

At the bottom, `word_n1` is catastrophic: a CER of 90.9% means the output is essentially unintelligible, and the 1.36 UTMOS confirms the audio itself has fallen apart. This collapse for the shortest units looks intrinsic rather than a training accident. At the top, `phone_n14` is genuinely good but still short of the baseline on every axis — CER 15.0% against 4.6%, SIM 0.844 against 0.901. The full reproduction confirmed those headline numbers across the ten speakers: over 330 conversions it averaged UTMOS 2.85, SIM 0.844, WER 25.5%, and CER 15.0%. Intelligibility varied widely by speaker — CER from about 8.7% for speaker 5339 up to roughly 20% for speakers 479 and 7789 — so the residual gap is not the work of a single bad talker.

## Next

The analysis leaves two threads open, both stated in `BASELINE_GAP.md`. One arm, `phone_n7_strict`, collapsed despite training to the same loss as its healthy sibling; it needs a single retrain to rule out a bad run before I trust the number. And now that the candidate causes of the residual gap are enumerated, the next document is the one this week's deliberately withheld: concrete fixes.
