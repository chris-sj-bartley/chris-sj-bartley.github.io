This week I settled the figure of the forced-alignment pipeline for my Interspeech 2026 paper, and I pushed on a question that has been shadowing the domain-adaptation work: can reshaping read speech to sound spontaneous actually help a recogniser handle spontaneous speech? The short answer, after a clean test, is no.

## [Endangered Language ASR](/research/#endangered-language-asr)

A recogniser learns from speech that has been cut into short clips, each paired with the words spoken in it. Endangered languages rarely come with data in that form; what survives is long recordings and, if you are lucky, a transcript of the whole thing. Forced alignment bridges the gap: it walks the transcript against the audio and pins each word to its moment, turning an hour-long recording into hundreds of usable training clips.

My Interspeech 2026 paper describes a Kaldi-based pipeline that does this for long-form transcribed speech. The transcript is normalised and compiled into *biased* language models — grammars that expect the words actually in the transcript, which keeps the search on track over long stretches — while the audio is reduced to MFCC features, a compact summary of its sound. A lexicon and a seed acoustic model then let a decoder find the alignment. The figure below contrasts two ways of supplying those last two ingredients. The earlier approach of Panayotov et al. (2015) leans on an English pronunciation dictionary and a ready-made acoustic model. Mine instead builds a graphemic lexicon straight from the writing system and bootstraps its own acoustic model from a handful of short clips — monophone, then triphone — so the method needs neither a pronunciation dictionary nor a pre-trained model in the target language, which is exactly what an endangered language lacks.

## Other

**Does voice conversion help a recogniser adapt?** Spontaneous speech — meetings, conversations — is hard for recognisers trained mostly on read speech, and spontaneous recordings are scarce. One tempting shortcut is *voice conversion*: take the plentiful read speech of LibriSpeech (people reading audiobooks aloud) and digitally reshape it to sound like the spontaneous AMI meeting corpus, then train on the result. I ran a controlled test of that idea. I fine-tuned a small Whisper recogniser for a single epoch on short three-word spans and measured its character error rate — the fraction of characters it gets wrong — on AMI meeting speech.

| Fine-tuning data | Hours | Held-out (270) | AMI 3-word (24,310) |
|---|--:|--:|--:|
| Real AMI spans (tight cuts) | 20.8 | 24.50 | 41.85 |
| Real AMI spans (random) | 21.3 | 20.27 | 23.72 |
| Real LibriSpeech | 21.3 | 14.79 | 28.13 |
| LibriSpeech converted to AMI style | 20.8 | 14.10 | 27.95 |
| Converted, then filtered | 8.7 | 10.89 | 18.88 |
| Real AMI + real LibriSpeech | 42.2 | 15.25 | 34.88 |
| Real AMI + converted LibriSpeech | 41.6 | 20.83 | 37.81 |

Character error rate (%); lower is better. Converting LibriSpeech to AMI style changed almost nothing: 27.95 against 28.13 for the untouched recordings, so the conversion added no useful variety. Used to pad out real AMI data, it actively hurt, pushing error from 34.88 up to 37.81. The one row that improved — 18.88 — came from keeping only the converted clips that an untrained recogniser already transcribes well. But that filter selects precisely the easy, faithful clips and throws away the garbled ones, so it is really removing bad conversions, not proving that conversion helps.

**Why the conversion fails.** I traced the problem to the signal itself. Converted LibriSpeech has a character error rate of 15.46% when fed straight to the recogniser, against 2.11% for the originals. Passing the real audio through the same final synthesis stage still scored 2.11%, so that stage is innocent; the damage happens earlier, where the model predicts the sound. The converted speech comes out flat — a pitch range of 4.5 semitones versus 12.8 for real LibriSpeech — over-smoothed, and collapsed toward a single generic voice. The recogniser is being handed degraded audio before it ever starts.

**Measuring spontaneity.** To pin down what actually separates spontaneous from read speech, I built three measures rooted in Lindblom's hyper- and hypo-articulation theory — the idea that speakers tighten or relax their articulation to suit the moment. The three are articulation rate (sounds produced per second of real speech), vowel-space size (how far vowels drift toward a neutral centre), and a coarticulation index (how much neighbouring sounds bleed together). I validated them on the Expresso corpus. One honest limit: because alignment forces the dictionary pronunciation onto the audio, outright dropped sounds go uncounted.

I also profiled thirteen English corpora along the read-to-spontaneous continuum, counting speaking pace, pauses, and *discourse markers* — the fillers of unplanned speech such as "uh," "um," "like," and "you know."
