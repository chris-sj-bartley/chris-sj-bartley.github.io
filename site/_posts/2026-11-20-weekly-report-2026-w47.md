This week I advanced the Interspeech 2026 paper on aligning endangered-language recordings, and did planning and groundwork on a compliance-automation venture and on a study of how read and spontaneous speech diverge.

## [Endangered Language ASR](/research/#endangered-language-asr)

Much of a language's recorded heritage survives as long recordings paired with a transcript but no timing: no record of where in the audio each word falls. Forced alignment supplies that timing. It walks the transcript against the sound and marks the boundaries of every word and segment. For a language with little training data, those aligned segments are the raw material for building a speech recogniser, so the alignment has to hold up even when the usual resources are absent.

The Interspeech 2026 paper builds this alignment on Kaldi, and the figure below contrasts two ways of doing it. The standard recipe, after Panayotov and colleagues' 2015 work on LibriSpeech, leans on two English-specific resources: the CMU pronunciation dictionary, which maps each word to a sequence of phonemes, and a triphone acoustic model pre-built from Voxforge recordings. Neither exists for most endangered languages. My approach replaces both. A graphemic lexicon is read straight off the writing system — letters serve as the units, so no pronunciation dictionary is needed — and a seed acoustic model is bootstrapped from a small amount of short-form data, passing through a monophone stage and then a bootstrapped-triphone stage. Both feed a decoder that, together with acoustic features from the waveform and language models biased toward the transcript, produces the alignment.

## Other

I adopted a build plan for a venture that automates language-heavy compliance work for regulated finance and gambling firms on the Isle of Man, starting with source-of-wealth review — the task of assembling and justifying evidence that a client's money came from legitimate origins. The guiding finding is that the language model is the smallest part of such a system. Its answers are only trustworthy if it is handed the right documents, keeps extracted facts in a persistent store tagged with their provenance rather than rebuilding them for each review, applies the firm's compliance rules in ordinary code rather than leaving them to the model's judgement, and ties every sentence it writes back to a specific document and page. The model itself is treated as replaceable: a mid-sized open-weight system that runs on a single rented graphics card, with no reliance on big-tech cloud services, since the intended customer is exactly the firm unwilling to put client files into cloud AI. I reordered the planned benchmark to tackle source of wealth first, on one synthetic trust company, before extending to the other workflows. The next step is to rework the pitch deck ahead of three advisor meetings.

On a separate thread I pushed forward a study of how read and spontaneous speech differ — the gap that makes a recogniser trained on scripted recordings stumble on natural conversation. I organised the differences into a taxonomy and tied the acoustic measures to Lindblom's hyper- and hypo-articulation theory, which holds that speakers slide along a continuum from careful, fully articulated speech to relaxed, reduced speech according to how much effort the situation demands; read speech sits toward the careful end, spontaneous speech toward the reduced. Among the measures is vowel undershoot — the tendency of vowels in quick or casual speech to fall short of their canonical targets — which I examined against segment duration, alongside work on composing the individual measures into a single combined score. I set aside four corpora (CHiME-6, SBCSAE, DiPCo and ICSI) whose recording-channel differences would have confounded the comparison, rather than trying to correct for them.
