A light week for the science: I ran no new experiments and have no fresh numbers to report. The work went into two places instead — building the pipeline that drafts these weekly reports, and preparing a figure for the Interspeech 2026 submission.

## Infrastructure

Most of the week's commits stand up an automated weekly-report pipeline in the site repository. Every Friday a GitHub Action gathers the week's material from three sources — commits and pull requests from GitHub, writing changes from Overleaf's git bridge, and any figures I tag in the LaTeX — then drafts a report in this style and opens a pull request for me to review before anything publishes.

Two design choices took most of the effort. First, the pipeline now tracks state between runs, recording the last run's time and hashes of the Overleaf content, so each report covers exactly what changed since the previous one rather than restating old work or missing edits. Second, figures are handled by intent rather than by compilation: instead of building the paper's TikZ, the drafting step reads the figure source at a high level and redraws it as a clean PNG with Graphviz or matplotlib, then inspects the result before embedding it. I also made cross-posting to the Gaelg AI blog opt-in per post, so a personal weekly report never propagates there unless I choose it.

## Writing

Overleaf shows no prose changes to the Interspeech 2026 paper this week. The one item I tagged for the report is the paper's system figure: the Kaldi-based pipeline for force-aligning long-form transcribed speech. *Force alignment* is the task of finding, for a known transcript, exactly when each word or sound occurs in the audio.

The figure below reproduces that pipeline. Two input streams meet at a decoder: the transcript is normalised and turned into biased language models, while the speech is reduced to MFCC features — a compact summary of the audio's short-term spectrum. The decoder also draws on a *lexicon*, which maps words to their sounds, and on a *seed acoustic model* that gives it an initial sense of what each sound looks like. The figure contrasts my approach (green) with the recipe of Panayotov et al., 2015 (blue), the LibriSpeech alignment method. Where they use the CMU pronunciation dictionary and a triphone model pre-built on VoxForge, I build a graphemic lexicon directly from the normalised text and bootstrap the acoustic model from short-form data through a monophone stage to a triphone. The decoder combines all of this and emits the forced alignment.
