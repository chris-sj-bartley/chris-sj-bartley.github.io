This week split between two threads: drafting the Interspeech 2026 paper on aligning long-form recordings in low-resource languages, and extending the accent- and style-conversion experiments with a shorter pairing rung and a leaner results table.

## [Minimally Supervised Endangered Language ASR](/projects/#minimally-supervised-endangered-language-asr)

I continued drafting the Interspeech 2026 paper, whose core is a pipeline for *forced alignment*: given a recording and a transcript of what is said, the system pinpoints exactly when each word is spoken. Endangered languages have plenty of transcribed archival audio, but the transcripts float free of the timeline, so the recordings cannot be used to train a speech recogniser. Alignment fixes that. The hard part is doing it with minimal supervision — without the hand-built pronunciation dictionary and large pretrained acoustic model that mainstream systems assume, since neither exists for most of these languages.

The figure below lays out the design. Two inputs enter on the left: the transcript, which is normalised and turned into biased language models that expect the words actually written down, and the long-form audio, from which acoustic features (MFCCs) are extracted. A decoder fuses the two, guided by a lexicon and a seed acoustic model, and emits the alignment. The contribution is in what feeds the decoder. Where earlier work leans on off-the-shelf resources — a phonetic pronouncing dictionary and a pre-built triphone model trained on read English — this pipeline substitutes ingredients that any language with a transcript can supply: a *graphemic* lexicon built straight from spelling, and a seed acoustic model bootstrapped from a small amount of short recordings, climbing from monophone to a bootstrapped triphone model. That substitution is what makes the method portable.

## Other

The rest of the week went to accent and style conversion — reshaping a recording so it carries a different regional accent, or a different speaking style, while keeping the words and, where possible, the voice.

I added a third rung to both conversion ladders. The models train on paired speech segments, and I sweep how long those segments are, measured in words; alongside the existing five- and ten-word rungs, I began a three-word rung on both the accent (VCTK) and style (Expresso) axes. The accent rung is sizeable: 179,534 segments drawn from 35,502 source utterances, 38.2 hours of audio, averaging 0.77 seconds each. The corpus-level machinery — the clustering codebooks, the quality gates, the held-out speaker lists — is shared across rungs and was not rebuilt.

For the accent axis I also settled the results table. It now reports seven columns instead of eleven: I dropped a native-accent word-error-rate column that duplicated claims made elsewhere, and collapsed three overlapping rhythm measures, keeping rhoticity (how strongly post-vocalic /r/ is pronounced, which cleanly separates England English from Scottish, Irish and North American) and a vowel-space measure. I generated the table with and without a single anomalous speaker, and at two test-set sizes (574 and 778 pairs), to check the numbers hold up.

The current accent-conversion standings, judged by an automatic accent classifier and a naturalness predictor, are below. "Accent → target" is how often the classifier labels the output as the intended accent (higher is better); "Accent → source" is how often it still hears the original (lower is better). Speaker similarity is a cosine to the source voice — here it measures timbre leakage rather than success, since source and target are different speakers. UTMOS is a predicted naturalness score from 1 to 5.

| Arm | Accent → target (%) | Accent → source (%) | Speaker sim. | UTMOS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Input (unconverted) | 6.8 | 84.5 | 1.00 | 4.04 |
| Real target-accent audio | 87.5 | 5.6 | 0.68 | 4.02 |
| Copy-synthesis (vocoder ceiling) | 81.9 | 7.0 | 0.68 | 3.75 |
| Model, 5-word pairs | 78.7 | 8.0 | 0.69 | 3.25 |
| Model, 10-word pairs | 74.4 | 10.3 | 0.69 | 3.01 |

The converted speech lands most of the way to genuine target-accent audio — around three-quarters to four-fifths of clips read as the intended accent, against 87.5% for the real thing and under 7% for the untouched input. The shorter five-word pairs edge out the ten-word pairs on both accent accuracy and naturalness, which is the trend the new three-word rung is meant to test.

Separately, a follow-up on the model's decoder came back affirmative, alongside diagnostics on the read-versus-spontaneous speech arm.

Next: completing the three-word rung needs the style-axis assets that do not yet exist — its clip inventory and the downstream features and configs — since that axis starts further back than the accent one.
