This week split between a submission figure for the Interspeech paper on aligning endangered-language recordings and a run of voice-conversion experiments, measurements, and write-ups for the domain-adaptation work.

## [Endangered Language ASR](/research/#endangered-language-asr)

When you have a recording and already know roughly what was said, *forced alignment* pins each word to its moment in the audio — it turns a loose transcript into a time-stamped one. The standard recipes for this lean on resources that only exist for well-served languages: a hand-built pronunciation dictionary and a large acoustic model trained on hundreds of hours of English. An endangered language has neither.

The pipeline I am describing in the Interspeech paper replaces both. Instead of the English CMU pronunciation dictionary it uses a *graphemic lexicon* — pronunciations read straight off the spelling — and instead of a pre-built English acoustic model it bootstraps one from a small amount of short, already-aligned speech, training a simple monophone model and then a richer "triphone" model that accounts for neighbouring sounds. Both the lexicon and the acoustic model feed a Kaldi decoder, which, together with language models biased toward the known transcript and standard acoustic features from the audio, emits word-level timings for long-form recordings. The figure below contrasts this route (green) with the conventional one that depends on the CMU dictionary and an off-the-shelf triphone model (blue).

## Other

The larger thread here is domain adaptation. A speech recogniser trained on clean, carefully read speech stumbles on spontaneous conversation — the hesitations, the pace, the overlapping chatter. I am testing whether *voice conversion* — reshaping read speech so it sounds spontaneous — can adapt a recogniser without new labelled conversation.

To make "spontaneous" something I can order rather than assert, I measured how spontaneous thirteen English corpora actually are. From a sixty-minute sample of each, an automatic transcriber (WhisperX) gave me three families of cues: speaking rate in words per minute; pauses (how often, how long, and what fraction of time they occupy); and *discourse markers* — the *uh*, *um*, *like*, *you know*, *I mean*, *well*, *oh* that pepper unplanned talk — counted per hundred words. The final column reports the transcriber's own word error rate against each corpus's reference, a proxy for how far to trust the automatic cues.

The discourse-marker count separates the corpora most sharply. Read material — TIMIT, Fleurs, LibriSpeech — sits below half a marker per hundred words; the conversational corpora (Switchboard, EdAcc, AMI) run from 2.5 to 3.5, with faster speech and longer pauses to match. That ranking is what orders the test sets from read to spontaneous in the adaptation experiments.

On the conversion model itself, the read-to-spontaneous system works in two stages: an encoder predicts a rough acoustic target (a "prior"), and a flow-matching decoder renders that target into a full spectrogram, with a separate model setting phone durations. I score how well the converted audio keeps the intended words by running Whisper large-v3 over it and measuring character error rate (CER) against the words it was meant to say — lower means content better preserved, pooled across clips rather than averaged per clip.

Fine-tuning the decoder on top of a frozen prior brought the intrinsic CER to 24.6, down from 27.2 at an earlier checkpoint. Training the encoder and decoder jointly from scratch did better still at a matched early checkpoint, and on one AMI evaluation clip it reproduced the target phrase ("IT SHOULD BE") exactly — CER 0 — at a predicted naturalness (UTMOS, an automatic 1-to-5 mean-opinion score) of 3.79, edging past the 3.67 of the genuine recording. I also caught that the conversion runs had been training on both directions at once — read-to-spontaneous and its reverse, 2.4 million pairs, half of them the wrong way — when the task is read-to-spontaneous only; the affected runs are being re-read against that.

A separate check settled a baseline question. A Grad-TTS-based converter (Grad-VC) reached CER 49.61 at epoch 10 when it averaged the speaker reference embeddings, and 76.20 with a single reference — both far short of the earlier DiffVC baseline's 6-to-11, so Grad-VC does not currently beat DiffVC.

On the writing side, the methods write-up gained architecture diagrams for Grad-VC and for the joint read-to-spontaneous model, and the domain-adaptation section now carries the spontaneity table above alongside a results table, still being filled, that will compare recognisers fine-tuned on clean read speech with and without voice-conversion adaptation across the read-to-spontaneous test sets.
