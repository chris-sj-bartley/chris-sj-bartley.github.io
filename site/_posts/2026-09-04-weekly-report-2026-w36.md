This week ran across two papers — a force-alignment pipeline for recognising endangered languages, and a broadened argument about converting the attributes of speech from short parallel segments — together with the evaluation and training machinery that will let me measure accent conversion.

## [Minimally Supervised Endangered Language ASR](/projects/#minimally-supervised-endangered-language-asr)

The endangered-language work this week went into a single figure for the Interspeech 2026 paper, which lays out how the system aligns long-form transcribed speech. Force alignment is the task of taking a recording together with its written transcript and finding exactly where in the audio each word — and each individual speech sound — falls. It matters because a language with only a few hours of recordings cannot afford to waste any of them: once every word is pinned to its moment in time, long recordings can be cut into the clean, labelled fragments that train a recogniser.

![Force-alignment pipeline for long-form transcribed speech](/assets/weekly-reports/2026-W36/interspeech-2026-1.png)

Two ingredients meet in a decoder. The transcript is normalised and turned into constrained language models that expect only the words actually spoken, while the audio is reduced to the acoustic features (MFCCs) that describe its spectrum over time. A *lexicon* spells each word out as a sequence of sounds, and a *seed acoustic model* supplies rough models of what those sounds look like; the decoder marries all four and reads off the timing.

The figure contrasts my choices (green) with the well-known LibriSpeech recipe of Panayotov et al. (2015, blue). Where that recipe leans on a curated pronunciation dictionary and a triphone acoustic model built in advance, mine avoids both — luxuries an endangered language rarely has. In their place it uses a graphemic lexicon, which treats a word's letters as stand-ins for its sounds, and it bootstraps its own acoustic model from a small amount of short-form data, training a simple monophone model first and then a triphone model on top of it.

## [Domain Adaptation](/projects/#domain-adaptation)

The ICASSP 2027 paper grew this week from a study of voice conversion into a broader argument about *speech attributes conversion*: changing some property of a recording — who appears to be speaking (their timbre), their accent, or their emotional and speaking style — while leaving the words themselves untouched. These are usually treated as three separate problems, and all three run into the same wall: to supervise a conversion model directly you need parallel recordings, two utterances of the very same words differing only in the target attribute, and such pairs are assumed not to exist in any quantity. That assumption is why so much of the field turns to unsupervised methods.

The paper challenges the assumption. Sliding a window of fixed length across transcripts, it harvests short segments — sub-utterances — that happen to share the same words across different speakers, accents, and styles, and pairs them into parallel corpora. The paper reports that workable timbre conversion can be trained from windows as short as five phones, roughly 0.44 seconds of speech on average, and it sets out how much more context accent and style each demand before conversion becomes reliable. It then carries the same idea into domain conversion, using it to augment the training data of a speech recogniser. The retitling and new abstract reflect this wider framing, with sub-utterance pairing offered as a general recipe for manufacturing parallel supervision.

## Other

Most of the week's code went into the evaluation and training scaffolding for accent conversion — recasting a speaker's accent as another while keeping their voice and their words fixed.

I re-froze the accent benchmark around accent labels assigned by people rather than predicted by a machine. Human labels are the scarcer resource, and that scarcity propagates into the benchmark. The eight accents are chosen by how much labelled test data supports them: US (1,276 utterances), Indian (473), England (337), Canadian (116), Australian (97), Southern African (52), Scottish (35), and Irish (33). Because the set has to stay balanced across accents, the rarest — Irish, with only 33 usable utterances — caps them all. The frozen benchmark accordingly shrank from 1,120 pairs to 672: eight accents, seven target accents each, twelve utterances apiece.

I also laid out an *accent ladder*, a series of training runs that vary only how much shared context each parallel pair carries. The rungs are cross-accent pairs of VCTK recordings at five window lengths — 3, 5, 7, 10, and 14 words — under a common ceiling of 5,000 audio-hours of training, drawn from 94 speakers with 21 more held out for evaluation. The longer the window, the rarer a matching pair becomes, and the trade-off is stark:

| Window (words) | Ordered pairs | Clips | Distinct word sequences | Est. training hours |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|

The rungs and their reference anchors are in place, but the conversion models along the ladder have not yet been trained and scored.

Having genuinely parallel text also let me add a paired distance measure. Earlier, with no matching sentences to compare against, the gap between a conversion and its target accent could only be measured distributionally, against the spread of real target-accent speech. Now the reference can be the target recording itself: a perfect conversion would reproduce that recording exactly and so score zero, which makes zero the definition of the ideal rather than something to measure against. The meaningful ceiling becomes the vocoder row instead — the same speaker and words passed through the synthesis pipeline — the best score the pipeline itself allows.

Finally, the parallel style-conversion results gained rows reporting the best development checkpoint measured on the test set, columns describing rhythm, and an optional duration-control setting.
