Two threads this week: a conference-paper figure explaining how we align long recordings of an endangered language to their transcripts, and the measurement machinery for a voice-conversion study that treats accent and speaking style as two separate axes.

## [Minimally Supervised Endangered Language ASR](/projects/#minimally-supervised-endangered-language-asr)

Work on the Interspeech 2026 paper centred on the diagram that lays out how we align long recordings to their transcripts. To train a speech recogniser for a language with little transcribed audio, you first have to know where in each long recording every word is spoken — a word-by-word alignment in time between the audio and its transcript. Force alignment is the standard way to get one: given the transcript and the audio, a model searches for the most likely placement of every word.

The figure contrasts our approach (green) with the established recipe from the LibriSpeech corpus (blue, Panayotov et al., 2015). Both feed a decoder that combines acoustic features — MFCCs, a compact summary of the short-term spectrum — with a pronunciation lexicon and a seed acoustic model, and read the alignment off the result. Where the standard recipe leans on a hand-curated pronunciation dictionary and an acoustic model pre-built from other data, ours builds a graphemic lexicon straight from the written text and bootstraps its own seed model from short-form recordings — a monophone model first, then a bootstrapped triphone. That sidesteps the curated pronunciation dictionary an endangered language is unlikely to have.

## Other

The rest of the week went to a voice-conversion study — converting a recording along one attribute while leaving the words intact — and, in particular, to how its results are measured. The study runs two axes in parallel: speaking style and accent. I built a single evaluation suite that scores both, deliberately sharing the rhythm, alignment, and reference-point code between them, so that any gap between the two results tables reflects a difference between the corpora, not between two measuring pipelines.

The accent axis raised a real methodological problem. Its corpus, GLOBE, contains no parallel text at all: across its 723,532 utterances, every sentence appears exactly once. That rules out the most direct way to score a conversion — comparing it against a genuine recording of the same sentence in the target accent — because no such recording can exist. Accent travels with a speaker's identity, and no one is recorded speaking the same words in someone else's accent. Every accent metric therefore has to be either a distance to the pool of real target-accent audio or a comparison back to the source.

Two measures change character as a result. Rhythm is rate-normalised on the accent axis but not on the style axis, using measures of how much successive vowel durations vary, because here the rhythm class itself is the cue — Indian English, for instance, is markedly more syllable-timed than US or England English. Speaker similarity flips role too: on the style axis the speaker is held fixed, so the measure was flat and I dropped it; on the accent axis nothing holds the speaker fixed, so the same number instead tracks how much of the original voice leaks through — a diagnostic to watch, not a score to maximise.

I also weighed a cleaner-labelled corpus, VCTK, as a possible home for the accent axis. Its appeal is genuine: accent labels are per-speaker self-reports rather than machine guesses, and it carries explicit speaker identities, where GLOBE's test labels are about four-fifths machine-imputed and agree with speakers' own reports only 62% of the time, against 94% on the training split. But 110 speakers and 42 hours are a narrow base, and on balance VCTK does not make a better home for the axis. I recorded the reasoning so the question stays settled rather than re-argued later, and kept VCTK on hand as a fallback.

With the suite in place, I measured the anchor rows that bracket the accent table — the reference conditions every converted result will be read against:

| Condition | As target accent | As source accent | Speaker similarity | Naturalness (UTMOS) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Source audio | 5% | 68% | 1.00 | 2.9 |
| Real target-accent audio | 68% | 5% | 0.63 | 2.9 |
| Target, copy-synthesised | 69% | 5% | 0.64 | 2.7 |

The accent classifier separates the two poles cleanly — source audio reads as 68% source accent and 5% target, real target audio the mirror image — which is what a trustworthy measuring stick should do. Passing the target audio through the vocoder alone (copy-synthesis) costs almost no accent but a little naturalness, marking the ceiling any conversion can reach. The converted arms themselves are still to be run.

Two smaller pieces of upkeep rounded out the week. The earlier voice-conversion reproduction had its naturalness metric changed: the second-generation automatic naturalness predictor it had used was retired, so I re-measured every existing arm with the first-generation predictor instead. The two are different models rather than two scales of one, and mixing them in a single table would be meaningless; re-measuring keeps every row comparable. Separately, a further experiment is under way to measure how well the automatic pairing recovers the parallel supervision that the Expresso corpus was designed to provide.
