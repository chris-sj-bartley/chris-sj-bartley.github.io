## [Endangered Language ASR](/research/#endangered-language-asr)

I spent the week on the Interspeech 2026 paper, whose central problem is deceptively simple: how do you turn the recordings an endangered-language community already owns into training data for a speech recogniser?

Modern speech recognition learns from short, transcribed clips — a few seconds of audio paired with the exact words spoken. Endangered languages rarely have these. What they do have is longer material: broadcasts, interviews, readings, each accompanied by a transcript that says *what* was spoken but not *when*. Forced alignment closes that gap. It pins every word to its moment in the audio, cutting a long recording into the short, time-stamped utterances a recogniser can be trained on.

The difficulty is that the standard alignment recipe quietly assumes two things a small language lacks: a hand-curated pronunciation dictionary that maps each word to its sounds, and an acoustic model already trained on many hours of the language. The paper's pipeline replaces both with resources an endangered language can actually supply.

![Kaldi-based pipeline for force-aligning long-form transcribed speech, contrasting the approach in this paper with a conventional LibriSpeech-style recipe.](/assets/weekly-reports/2026-W42/interspeech-2026-1.png)

The figure traces the flow. The transcript is normalised and compiled into biased language models that expect only the words known to have been spoken; the audio is reduced to MFCC features, a compact numerical summary of the sound. Both streams meet at a decoder, which produces the alignment. The departures from the conventional recipe sit in the two supporting boxes. In place of a curated pronunciation dictionary, the pipeline derives a *graphemic* lexicon straight from spelling, so no dictionary needs to exist beforehand. In place of a ready-made acoustic model, it bootstraps a seed model from a sliver of short-form data — the single-word entries of a spoken dictionary — training a simple monophone model and then a more detailed triphone one. The conventional baseline it is measured against, shown in blue, instead reaches for a general pronunciation dictionary and a pre-built acoustic model trained on unrelated English speech.

## Other

Most of the week's visible output was a reworking of my research website. I renamed the former "Projects" page to "Research" and narrowed it to the research problems themselves, redirecting the old address so existing links keep working, and I rewrote the home page as a personal narrative of the PhD so far. Its centrepiece is now an interactive timeline: a progress bar spanning 2025 to 2028 with clickable milestones — among them the first-year highlights, a confirmation review, the UKIS 2026 poster, and the Interspeech 2026 submission — each placed at its proper point on the bar.

I also published two posts. One recounts presenting *Bootstrapping Endangered Language ASR with Short-Form Corpora* at Interspeech 2026 in Sydney, a paper that tests whether the resources endangered-language communities already hold — spoken dictionaries and loosely transcribed long recordings — can build speech recognition for five languages: Cornish, Hawaiian, Jejueo, Manx, and Mohawk. The other introduces a searchable corpus of spoken Manx: type a word or phrase and jump to every point in 353 hours of community recordings where it is spoken, making decades of audio searchable for the first time. Finally, I linked the Interspeech paper's permanent archive entry from my publication list.
