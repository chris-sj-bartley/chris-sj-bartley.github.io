This week centered on two papers — one on lining up long recordings with their transcripts in languages that have almost no resources, one on teaching a voice-conversion model from fragments of speech that two speakers happen to share — and on a survey that pins thirteen English corpora along the line from read to spontaneous speech.

## [Minimally Supervised Endangered Language ASR](/projects/#minimally-supervised-endangered-language-asr)

The Interspeech submission builds around a pipeline that lines up a long recording with its transcript, deciding second by second which words are spoken when. This forced alignment is straightforward when a language has decades of curated resources and hard when it has almost none, which is the predicament of most endangered languages.

![Kaldi-based pipeline for force-aligning long-form transcribed speech.](/assets/weekly-reports/2026-W34/interspeech-2026-1.png)

The figure lays out the pipeline. The transcript is normalized and turned into biased language models that expect the words actually written down; the audio is reduced to MFCC features, a compact acoustic summary. A lexicon and a seed acoustic model then steer a decoder that emits the alignment. The contrast the paper draws lives in those two ingredients. The conventional route (Panayotov et al., 2015) leans on a hand-built pronunciation dictionary — the CMU dictionary — and an off-the-shelf triphone model trained on Voxforge. The approach here replaces both with resources a low-supervision language can actually supply: a graphemic lexicon that reads pronunciations straight off the spelling, and a seed acoustic model bootstrapped from a handful of short clips, passing through a monophone stage into a bootstrapped triphone.

## [Domain Adaptation](/projects/#domain-adaptation)

The second paper, aimed at ICASSP, asks how to train a voice-conversion model — a system that rewrites a recording to sound like a different speaker while keeping the words — without parallel recordings of the same sentence in two voices, which barely exist at scale. The idea is to manufacture the missing pairs from ordinary read speech: whenever two different speakers happen to utter the same short run of words or sounds, that shared fragment is a matched example, the same content in two voices. The paper calls these sub-utterance pairs.

How much supervision this yields depends on how long a fragment you insist on. I counted the matching fragments in the LibriTTS corpus. Short spans are plentiful and long spans scarce:

| Span (words) | Cross-speaker pairs | Mean duration (s) |
|---|---:|---:|
| 4 | 643,564 | 1.27 |
| 5 | 63,404 | 1.60 |
| 6 | 11,853 | 1.93 |
| 7 | 5,954 | 2.26 |
| 8 | 4,418 | 2.60 |

Four-word spans alone furnish more than six hundred thousand cross-speaker pairs, and even eight-word spans — running about two and a half seconds — supply several thousand. Matching at the level of individual speech sounds rather than whole words is more forgiving still: thirteen-phone spans yield 438,391 pairs. Supervision, in short, is not the scarce resource.

To see how far that supervision carries, I built a scaling study: a ladder that feeds the converter ever more paired audio and tracks two numbers against the hours of paired audio seen — speaker similarity, whether the output sounds like the target voice, and character error rate, whether the words survive the conversion intact.

A round of head-to-head screens has also closed. The variant that supplies alignment priors from a monotonic search won its comparison, reaching a character error rate of 10.12%, and now serves as the control for the next block of experiments. With the screens settled, the paired-data training runs are ready: feature preparation is complete — about 149,700 mel-spectrogram and speaker-embedding examples with some 49,000 matched alignment priors — both trainers have passed their smoke tests, and their budgets are fixed against the released baseline model of roughly 378,000 training steps. To keep the comparison clean, a from-scratch control was dropped in favor of measuring the released model against two pair-trained runs.

Next: launch those two pair-trained voice-conversion runs.

## Other

The last strand is a survey that places thirteen English speech corpora on a single axis: how scripted or spontaneous the talk is. On a random hour of each, I measured speaking rate, three facets of pausing, and the rate of discourse markers — the little words that pepper unplanned speech (*uh*, *um*, *like*, *you know*, *I mean*, *well*, *oh*), counted per hundred words. The final column, a word error rate from an automatic transcriber, is not the point of the table; it is a rough gauge of how far to trust the automatic measurements for each corpus. Faster speech, more and longer pauses, and more discourse markers all tend to mark spontaneity.

| Fleurs en | 184.8 ± 39.3 | 8.0 | 0.49 | 6.6% | 0.25 | 9.1% |
| CommonVoice en | 172.7 ± 54.2 | 7.1 | 0.43 | 5.1% | 0.42 | 11.5% |
| VoxPopuli en | 174.5 ± 53.6 | 17.7 | 0.47 | 13.8% | 0.31 | 8.2% |
| FLoras-50 | 198.9 ± 65.3 | 2.1 | 0.47 | 1.6% | 0.44 | 4.6% |
| LibriSpeech Clean | 206.0 ± 61.3 | 11.8 | 0.47 | 9.2% | 0.47 | 2.1% |
| Earnings-22 | 178.5 ± 73.6 | 17.5 | 0.62 | 18.0% | 0.70 | 14.6% |

The discourse-marker column separates the corpora most sharply. Scripted read-aloud sets — TIMIT, LibriSpeech, Fleurs — sit near a fifth of a marker per hundred words; open conversation and meetings run more than ten times higher, with Switchboard at 3.54, Edinburgh-accented conversation at 2.54, and AMI meetings at 2.51. That ordering, from read to spontaneous, is exactly the axis the domain-adaptation experiments arrange their test sets along.
