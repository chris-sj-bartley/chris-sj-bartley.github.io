This week split between two threads: writing up a method for aligning transcripts to long recordings of an endangered language, and pushing the voice-conversion experiments that reshape clean read speech into something that sounds spontaneous.

## [Endangered Language ASR](/research/#endangered-language-asr)

To train a speech recogniser you need recordings cut into short clips, each paired with the words spoken in it. For an endangered language the raw material is often the opposite: a few hours of continuous audio with a running transcript but no indication of *when* each word is said. Recovering those timings — lining the transcript up against the waveform so you can slice the audio at word boundaries — is called forced alignment. It is the bottleneck between an archive of recordings and a usable training set.

I spent the week writing up the alignment method for the Interspeech 2026 paper, setting out the pipeline and its components while leaving the dataset descriptions for a later pass. The pipeline runs two streams in parallel. The transcript is normalised and turned into language models that are *biased* toward the known words, so the decoder is only ever choosing among things the speaker actually said. The audio, meanwhile, is reduced to MFCCs — a compact acoustic summary of each short frame of sound. A decoder then reconciles the two, using a lexicon and an acoustic model to place every word and phone on the timeline.

![Kaldi-based pipeline for force-aligning long-form transcribed speech](/assets/weekly-reports/2026-W43/interspeech-2026-1.png)

Two choices separate this approach from the standard recipe (Panayotov et al., 2015), shown in blue above. The standard recipe leans on resources that a well-studied language already has: a CMU-style pronunciation dictionary mapping words to phonemes, and an acoustic model pre-built from a large outside corpus. Neither exists for most endangered languages. In their place I use a *graphemic* lexicon — treating the written letters themselves as the sound units, so no phonetic dictionary is required — and a seed acoustic model bootstrapped from the target language's own short-form data, growing from a simple monophone model into a bootstrapped triphone one. The result is an alignment pipeline that needs only the recordings and their transcripts.

## Other

The second thread is domain adaptation for speech recognition. A recogniser trained on clean, carefully read audio stumbles on real conversation — the pace, the pauses, the *ums* and *you knows* all look unfamiliar. One way to close that gap is voice conversion: take read recordings and transform them so they carry the rhythm and texture of spontaneous speech, then use the converted audio to adapt the recogniser. The hard part is doing this without smearing the words beyond recognition.

To ground the work I first needed an objective way to say how spontaneous a corpus actually sounds. I measured a handful of proxies across thirteen English corpora: speaking rate in words per minute; three views of pausing (how often pauses occur, how long they last, and the fraction of total time spent paused); and the rate of discourse markers — the *uh*, *um*, *like*, *you know*, *I mean*, *well*, *oh* that pepper unplanned talk — per hundred words. An automatic transcription error rate accompanies each row as a rough confidence proxy on the measurements. Read corpora such as TIMIT and LibriSpeech sit at one end with few pauses and almost no discourse markers; conversational corpora such as Switchboard (SWBD), AMI meetings, and the Edinburgh accented-speech corpus (EdAcc) sit at the other, dense with both.

These numbers give the main experiment a backbone: the test sets can be laid out left to right along a continuum from read to spontaneous, and a recogniser's error rate tracked as it moves down that line, with and without voice-conversion adaptation. The comparison spans self-supervised models fine-tuned on a hundred hours of read LibriSpeech (wav2vec2, HuBERT, WavLM) alongside large multilingual models used as-is. AMI, the meeting corpus at the spontaneous end, is the target the conversion is aiming for.

Most of the week's compute went into converting LibriSpeech read speech into AMI-style speech and measuring the result two ways at once: how intelligible it stays, scored by the character error rate of an automatic transcription, and how spontaneous it has become, measured by the same pause and timing proxies as above. I assembled a single table that pits every conditioning variant against the others at a common training budget, so each improvement can be read off directly.

The clearest gain came from conditioning the conversion on speaker, channel, and style statistics. Against a content-loss-only control that managed 45.2% character error rate, adding those statistics cut the error to 26.1% — a large recovery of intelligibility, though it also pulled the pause rate down to 2.0 per minute, further from AMI's natural pacing rather than toward it. That tension — between sounding spontaneous and staying legible — ran through the rest of the work, which concentrated on *timing*: how long each sound is held, and how that lengthening is distributed.

Anchoring each phone to the duration it had in the source recording, rather than sampling a fresh duration, helped markedly. On one build it took the error rate from 24.6% down to 15.6% — but only at a read-speech pace of 188 words per minute, not the faster, more variable timing of real conversation. Carrying that idea further, a word-level, source-anchored timing model layered on the learned-style-encoder build reached 11.7% character error, the lowest so far. The remaining puzzle is that AMI's spontaneity comes partly from sparse, well-placed lengthening — a single sound held for more than a second before a pause — whereas the current timing model spreads small perturbations across every phone, which costs intelligibility without buying the right rhythm.

Next: retrain the timing model to put its stretching where AMI puts it — lengthening before pauses and the occasional long-held sound — by giving it word-level information about where those holds belong, and judge it against the source-length-at-AMI-rate result rather than against read-speech timing.
