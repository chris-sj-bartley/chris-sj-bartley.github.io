Two strands of the same problem this week: drafting the Interspeech 2026 paper on how to turn long recordings into usable training data, and assembling the Manx-language speech corpus that such methods are meant to feed on.

## [Minimally Supervised Endangered Language ASR](/projects/#minimally-supervised-endangered-language-asr)

I pushed forward the Interspeech 2026 paper, which tackles speech recognition for languages so short of resources that almost no transcribed audio exists to train on. The recordings that do exist tend to be long and only loosely matched to their transcripts, which makes them hard to learn from directly. The remedy is forced alignment: given a recording and the text of what is said in it, the system works out exactly when each word is spoken, slicing hours of continuous audio into the short, precisely timed segments a recogniser can be trained on.

The paper's central figure lays out the alignment pipeline, which is built on the Kaldi speech-recognition toolkit.

![Kaldi-based pipeline for force-aligning long-form transcribed speech](/assets/weekly-reports/2026-W40/interspeech-2026-1.png)

A transcript and its recording enter on the left. The text is normalised and used to build *biased* language models — decoding is steered toward the words we already know were said — while the audio is reduced to MFCC features, a compact summary of its sound. A decoder combines the two, drawing on a lexicon (how words are pronounced) and a seed acoustic model (what the sounds look like), and emits a time-aligned transcript.

The figure sets my approach (green) against the established LibriSpeech alignment recipe of Panayotov et al. (2015) (blue). The baseline leans on resources that an endangered language simply does not have: a hand-built English pronunciation dictionary and a ready-made acoustic model trained on crowd-sourced English. In its place I use a *graphemic* lexicon — pronunciations read straight off the spelling, so no curated dictionary is needed — and a seed acoustic model bootstrapped from a small amount of short-form data, progressing from a simple monophone model to a bootstrapped triphone one. Both routes end at the same decoder and the same alignment, but only the green route is feasible when the language has no dictionary and no pre-trained model to borrow.

## Other

Alongside the paper I began building a speech corpus for Manx, the Gaelic language of the Isle of Man, drawn from publicly available recordings that already carry transcripts — exactly the raw material the alignment pipeline above is meant to consume.

The first stage was inventory and provenance. I catalogued each candidate recording, the transcript it pairs with, and where it can be found online; checked that the stored audio lined up with its online source at the right offsets; flagged links that had broken; and set aside both the recordings reserved for testing and any audio under embargo, so that neither leaks into training. One podcast series, *Abbyr Shen Reesht*, needed its feed catalogued and sixty-three missing episodes backfilled.

With the audio in place I ran a first pass of recognition, comparing two systems: Whisper, a large general-purpose speech recogniser, and a time-delay neural network (TDNN) acoustic model trained in Kaldi. After calibrating the setup, I decoded across two GPUs and grouped the output into confidence bands, measuring each system's word error rate — the fraction of words it gets wrong — within them. For the lowest-confidence (red) segments, where the output is least trustworthy, the corpus takes its transcription from the TDNN system. The bands were reviewed and signed off.

Next: the corpus has now moved on to forced alignment — the stage the figure above depicts. I built and smoke-tested the alignment on a sample and measured its runtime, then launched the full run on CPU, with its quality checks to follow.
