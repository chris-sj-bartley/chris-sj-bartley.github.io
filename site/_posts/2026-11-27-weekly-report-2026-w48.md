This week split between a conference paper on aligning speech in low-resource languages and planning a venture to automate compliance paperwork on the Isle of Man.

## [Endangered Language ASR](/research/#endangered-language-asr)

I spent the week on the Interspeech 2026 paper, *Bootstrapping Endangered Language ASR with Short-Form Corpora*, refining the writing and settling the figure that lays out its central method: a pipeline for **forced alignment** of long-form recordings.

Forced alignment solves a mundane but blocking problem. Archives of endangered languages hold hours of interviews, broadcasts and folklore, but as continuous audio paired with transcripts that only loosely track what is said — no marker for where one utterance ends and the next begins. Modern speech recognisers need the opposite: a corpus of short, cleanly cut clips, each matched to its words. Forced alignment bridges the two. Given a recording and its transcript, it finds the point in the audio where each word falls, so a long recording can be sliced into training-ready fragments.

The difficulty is circular: aligning the audio needs an acoustic model, and training an acoustic model needs aligned audio. The figure below shows how the pipeline breaks the loop and how it departs from the conventional recipe (the blue path, after Panayotov et al.'s LibriSpeech work). Two inputs enter at the left — a transcript and the long-form speech. The transcript is normalised and turned into biased language models that expect its words; the audio is reduced to **MFCCs**, a compact summary of the sound. A Kaldi decoder then lines the two up and emits the alignment. Feeding that decoder are two supports the conventional recipe builds differently. For the **lexicon** — the map from written words to the sounds that pronounce them — the standard route reaches for a hand-built pronunciation dictionary, which most endangered languages do not have; the approach here derives a **graphemic lexicon** straight from spelling instead. And where the standard route borrows a ready-made acoustic model, here a small amount of short-form audio (isolated words and phrases) seeds one, trained up through monophone to a bootstrapped triphone model good enough to align the long recordings.

## Other

Alongside the research I worked on **Obbyr Flow** (*obbyr* is Manx for "work"), a venture to automate the language-heavy paperwork that regulated firms grind through by hand. The first market is Isle of Man firms bound by the island's anti-money-laundering rules — trust and corporate service providers, banks, life companies and gambling operators — and the first workflow is **source-of-wealth review**: assembling and justifying the evidence that a client's money came from where they say it did.

Most of the week went into adopting a build plan that fixes how such a firm would be served. The guiding finding is that the language model is the smallest part of the system; its answers are only trustworthy if it is handed the right documents, the firm's rules are applied explicitly rather than left to the model's judgement, and every sentence it writes can be traced to a source. So the design keeps compliance rules in ordinary code and asks the model only to extract facts and draft narratives; it stores those facts in a persistent, per-client store with a fixed schema and a record of where each came from; and it cites every claim down to the document and page, flagging any required item for which no evidence exists. The model itself is a mid-sized open-weight one, chosen to be replaceable.

I also settled the pitch, built around three claims a buyer can check for themselves:

- **Trust** — the system runs locally on Island hardware with open-weight models and no big-tech cloud, and logs every step so a firm can reconstruct exactly what the system saw and why.
- **Accuracy** — verifiable output, with every claim tied to a stored fact and every required item either met or flagged; a knowledge store linking the island's rules to clients, owners and evidence, kept current; and improvement with use, through a small per-client adapter (a LoRA) trained only on that firm's own cases and corrections.
- **Expertise** — grounding in the specific regulatory domain the work serves.

The accompanying pitch deck went through several rounds to bring it in line with the build plan, ahead of early advisor meetings.
