I'm Chris, a PhD researcher at the University of Sheffield. I'm developing data-efficient approaches to speech technology for languages left behind by modern technology, starting with Manx Gaelic.

About me

Since I was young, I have been fascinated by how a language can make no sense to me, yet be perfectly intelligible to others. This led me to the study of languages, through school, and later during my undergraduate degree in Modern Languages (Spanish, French, & Mandarin). I discovered that what I loved more than actually speaking the languages was the languages themselves, and how they reflect our culture and patterns of thought. I later realised that this was linguistics, leading me to study it at Master's level at the University of Manchester. Here, I refined my interests further, taking an interest in applied linguistics, and in particular, the intersection of linguistics and computer science.

I could see that the computational tools I was learning about could do real good for languages the technology industry mostly ignores, and that curiosity carried me into computer science. Today I'm a PhD researcher in the Speech and Hearing Research Group at Sheffield, where I develop computational methods to support the revitalisation of under-resourced and critically endangered languages.

Why endangered languages

Nearly half of the world's 7,000 or so languages are endangered. When a language falls silent, a community loses a way of seeing the world, along with stories, songs and knowledge that only ever existed in that language.

Manx is a good example of what is at stake, and of what is possible. Its last native speaker, Ned Maddrell, died in 1974. Thanks to decades of work by the community, Manx is spoken again today, taught in schools and used in a Manx-medium primary school. What it doesn't have is the technology that speakers of larger languages take for granted: speech recognition to unlock archive recordings, or synthesis to help learners hear how words should sound.

The obstacle is rarely a total lack of recordings. It is that the recordings exist in forms that modern speech technology isn't built to use. Most of my work comes back to that gap.

The story so far

  1. 2024

    Collecting Manx

    When I started, Manx had no speech recognition and no speech corpus of the kind such systems are trained on. So the first job was gathering what existed: spoken dictionary entries, broadcasts, interviews and folklore recordings, more than 300 hours of audio in all.

    Very little of it looked like a standard training corpus. Some was thousands of isolated words; the rest was long recordings with loosely matching transcripts. Working out how to turn that material into something a model could learn from became the starting point for everything that followed.

    More on the Manx speech corpus

  2. October 2025

    A first ASR system for Manx

    Using a spoken dictionary as the seed, I built the first speech recognition systems for Manx and for Cornish. Just 40 minutes of dictionary audio was enough for a usable baseline (under 50% word error rate), and as little as 8 minutes was enough to begin aligning the long-form recordings and turning them into new training data. With domain-matched segments added, the Manx system reached under 25% word error rate.

    Along the way the project produced the first utterance-level speech corpora for Manx (18 hours) and Cornish (39 hours). One lesson stood out: variety of speakers, styles and sources mattered more than sheer quantity of audio. I wrote the work up as a preprint.

  3. February 2026

    Scaling up to five languages

    The Manx results raised an obvious question: was this a lucky case, or something general? I set out to answer it in a paper submitted to Interspeech, Bootstrapping Endangered Language ASR with Short-Form Corpora. It contributes:

    • evidence, using English, that word-level recordings are a viable substitute for the sentence-level data ASR normally needs, with accuracy holding steady until segments drop below about a second;
    • the same short-form to long-form pipeline applied to five very different languages (Cornish, Hawaiian, Jejueo, Manx and Mohawk), with a new open corpus released for each;
    • systems trained entirely on CPU that beat zero-shot Omnilingual ASR, MMS and Whisper on out-of-domain tests in nearly every language, at a fraction of the compute.

    The short version: the barrier to entry for endangered-language ASR is much lower than commonly assumed, and the resources communities already create can be turned into working transcription tools.

  7. Now

    Tackling domain mismatch

    My current work picks up the biggest weakness the Interspeech paper exposed, using voice conversion to generate training data for the kinds of speech the systems struggle with. More on that below.

What's next: the domain problem

ASR works well on speech that resembles its training data and much worse on anything else. Endangered-language data is usually careful read speech from a few speakers, while real archives are full of spontaneous conversation. The Interspeech paper shows the cost: out-of-domain error is well above in-domain error in all five languages, and for Cornish it climbs from under 8% on read speech to over 70% on a spontaneous interview.

The usual fixes are collecting in-domain data, adapting models to the target domain, self-training on untranscribed audio, and data augmentation. Augmentation suits endangered languages best because it needs no new recordings, but standard techniques (speed changes, noise, spectrogram masking) alter the conditions around speech, not the way people actually speak.

My approach: converting speech between domains

I'm working on data augmentation through speech-to-speech conversion: taking the read speech a system already has and converting aspects of it (who is speaking, their accent, their speaking style) towards the domain the system will face, while keeping the words the same. The converted audio keeps its transcript, so it can go straight into training.

The difficulty is teaching a model to do the conversion. The cleanest way is to show it pairs of recordings that say the same thing but differ in one attribute. Whole sentences almost never repeat across speakers, so such pairs are widely assumed to be unobtainable, and most of the field turns to unsupervised methods instead. Those work well for changing a voice, but struggle with accent and style, which are about how something is said.

My answer is a sub-utterance pairing scheme. Rather than looking for whole matching sentences, I slide a short window over the transcripts of an ordinary corpus and match identical runs of words or phones spoken by different speakers. Short sequences recur constantly, so this recovers a large amount of real, paired training data from corpora that were never designed for it, with no extra recording or synthesis. Shorter windows give many more pairs but less context to learn from, and finding the balance between the two is part of the work.

Trained this way, diffusion-based conversion models handle speaker, accent and speaking-style conversion, and the converted speech can be used to train ASR across a range from read to spontaneous speech. This is the subject of the paper I'm currently preparing for ICASSP 2027.
