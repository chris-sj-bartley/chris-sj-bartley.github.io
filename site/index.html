I'm Chris, a PhD researcher at the University of Sheffield. I'm developing data-efficient approaches to speech technology for languages left behind by modern technology, starting with Manx Gaelic.

About me

Since I was young, I have been fascinated by how a language can make no sense to me, yet be perfectly intelligible to others. This led me to the study of languages, through school, and later during my undergraduate degree in Modern Languages (Spanish, French, & Mandarin). I discovered that what I loved more than actually speaking the languages was the languages themselves, and how they reflect our culture and patterns of thought. I later realised that this was linguistics, leading me to study it at Master's level at the University of Manchester. Here, I refined my interests further, taking an interest in applied linguistics, and in particular, the intersection of linguistics and computer science.

I could see that the computational tools I was learning about could do real good for languages the technology industry mostly ignores, and that curiosity carried me into computer science. Today I'm a PhD researcher in the Speech and Hearing Research Group at Sheffield, where I develop computational methods to support the revitalisation of under-resourced and critically endangered languages.

Why endangered languages

Nearly half of the world's 7,000 or so languages are endangered. When a language falls silent, a community loses a way of seeing the world, along with stories, songs and knowledge that only ever existed in that language.

Manx is a good example of what is at stake, and of what is possible. Its last native speaker, Ned Maddrell, died in 1974. Thanks to decades of work by the community, Manx is spoken again today, taught in schools and used in a Manx-medium primary school. What it doesn't have is the technology that speakers of larger languages take for granted: speech recognition to unlock archive recordings, or synthesis to help learners hear how words should sound.

The obstacle is rarely a total lack of recordings. It is that the recordings exist in forms that modern speech technology isn't built to use. Most of my work comes back to that gap.

The story so far

PhD: October 2024 to April 2028

  1. October – December 2024Starting the PhD and collecting Manx

    I started my PhD in October 2024. At that point Manx had no speech recognition and no speech corpus of the kind such systems are trained on, so the first job was gathering what existed: spoken dictionary entries, broadcasts, interviews and folklore recordings. By December I had around 300 hours of speech and 4.8 million words of text.

    Very little of it looked like a standard training corpus. Some was thousands of isolated words; the rest was long recordings with loosely matching transcripts. Working out how to turn that material into something a model could learn from became the starting point for everything that followed.

    More on this research

  2. January – March 2025Manx Forge and learning the tools

    In January I set up Manx Forge, a GitHub page intended as a one-stop shop for Manx language resources.

    To make use of the material I'd collected, I learnt Kaldi, the speech recognition toolkit, and forced alignment, the technique of lining up a transcript with the exact moments each word is spoken in a recording. Alignment became the key that unlocked the long recordings.

  3. February – June 2025The world's first Manx ASR

    Using a spoken dictionary as the seed, I built the first speech recognition systems for Manx in Kaldi. Just 40 minutes of dictionary audio was enough for a usable baseline (under 50% word error rate), and as little as 8 minutes was enough to begin aligning the long-form recordings and turning them into new training data. With domain-matched segments added, the system reached under 25% word error rate. One lesson stood out: variety of speakers, styles and sources mattered more than sheer quantity of audio.

  4. May 2025Getting out there

    I took the work on the road for the first time at UKIS, the annual UK and Ireland speech conference, and spent a lot of time talking to other researchers about what I was building.

  5. June – July 2025First demo and feedback

    I recorded the first public demonstration of Manx speech recognition, transcribing a local radio show, and spent July gathering feedback on what was useful and what wasn't.

  6. August 2025The world's first Cornish ASR

    To check the approach wasn't specific to Manx, I applied it to Cornish, producing the first speech recognition system for that language too. Together the two projects produced the first utterance-level speech corpora for Manx (18 hours) and Cornish (39 hours).

  7. September – October 2025First paper

    I wrote up the Manx and Cornish work and submitted it to ICASSP in September, then posted it as a preprint in October.

    More on this research

  8. November 2025Confirmation review

    I passed my confirmation review, the milestone that officially makes me a PhD candidate. I wrote a 50-page report that works like a mini thesis and defended it in front of a panel of experts.

  9. January 2026The first Manx text-to-speech

    I built the first text-to-speech system for Manx: type in Manx text and hear it read aloud. Watch the demo. It was covered by BBC News, Isle of Man Today and Digital Isle of Man.

  10. February 2026Submitted to Interspeech

    ICASSP didn't accept the first paper, so I reworked it for Interspeech and made it bigger. The Manx and Cornish results raised an obvious question: was this a lucky case, or something general? To find out, I added three more languages: Hawaiian, Jejueo (from Jeju Island in Korea) and Mohawk. Jejueo and Mohawk had never been supported by any speech recognition system before. I submitted the paper, Bootstrapping Endangered Language ASR with Short-Form Corpora, in February. It contributes:

    • evidence, using English, that word-level recordings are a viable substitute for the sentence-level data ASR normally needs, with accuracy holding steady until segments drop below about a second;
    • the same short-form to long-form pipeline applied to five very different languages (Cornish, Hawaiian, Jejueo, Manx and Mohawk), with a new open corpus released for each;
    • systems trained entirely on CPU that beat zero-shot Omnilingual ASR, MMS and Whisper on out-of-domain tests in nearly every language, at a fraction of the compute.

    The short version: the barrier to entry for endangered-language ASR is much lower than commonly assumed, and the resources communities already create can be turned into working transcription tools.

Things I've built

I want this research to end up in people's hands, not just in papers.

  • Gaelg AI is a free web platform of AI tools for Manx: text-to-speech, speech recognition, translation, and audio timestamping, all built on the models from my research. Read the launch post
  • A searchable speech corpus of Manx covers all 350+ hours of Manx speech on the web. Search by text and hear every place a word or phrase is spoken.
